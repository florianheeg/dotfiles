# Avoid generic AI-writing patterns

This is a local writing-quality reference. It is intentionally self-contained. Do not consult external pages for additional rules.

The goal is not to "beat" an AI detector. The goal is to avoid prose habits that make text feel generic, synthetic, over-produced, or unlike Flo.

## Avoid canned framing

Usually remove phrases such as:

- "It's important to note that..."
- "It's worth noting that..."
- "It is crucial to..."
- "In today's fast-paced..."
- "When it comes to..."
- "At the end of the day..."
- "In conclusion..."
- "In summary..."
- "Overall..." when it merely announces a conclusion
- "Let's dive into..."
- "Let's explore..."

Start with the actual point instead.

## Avoid inflated significance

Do not make ordinary workplace matters sound strategic, transformative, critical, exciting, powerful, or game-changing without a concrete reason.

Avoid words such as these when a simpler word works:

- leverage
- empower
- unlock
- elevate
- robust
- seamless
- transformative
- impactful
- holistic
- strategic
- cutting-edge
- dynamic
- ecosystem
- journey
- landscape
- pivotal

These words are not forbidden when technically correct. They are suspicious when they only add tone.

## Avoid artificial balance and symmetry

Do not force every explanation into:

- three bullets,
- three benefits,
- "not only X, but also Y",
- "whether X, Y, or Z",
- neat pairs of advantages and challenges,
- perfectly parallel sentence structures.

Human workplace writing is often uneven. Keep useful asymmetry.

## Avoid excessive headings

For short messages, emails, comments, and explanations, do not add headings unless the user asks for structure or the text genuinely needs it.

## Avoid repetitive rhetorical transitions

Do not repeatedly use transitions such as:

- furthermore
- moreover
- additionally
- consequently
- therefore
- however

Use them only when they improve clarity. Often a new sentence is enough.

## Avoid conclusion restatement

Do not repeat the same point in a final paragraph merely to create a polished ending. Stop when the message is complete.

## Avoid generic helpful-assistant endings

Do not append offers such as:

- "Let me know if you have any questions."
- "Hope this helps!"
- "Feel free to reach out."
- "I'm happy to help further."

Use them only when they are genuinely natural for the requested message.

## Avoid fake conversational warmth

Do not add:

- excessive exclamation marks,
- "Absolutely!",
- "Great question",
- unnecessary compliments,
- repeated thanks,
- exaggerated reassurance,
- forced empathy.

## Avoid overusing em dashes

Prefer commas, full stops, parentheses, or separate sentences. Use an em dash only when it is clearly the most natural punctuation.

## Avoid synonym churn

Do not replace repeated technical or workplace terms just to sound varied. If "server", "priority", "deployment", or "people lead" is the correct word, repeat it.

## Avoid vague abstraction

Prefer concrete statements:

"The deployment from an hour ago suddenly started returning the expected result."

not:

"The environment eventually aligned with the expected state."

## Avoid polishing away uncertainty

Keep uncertainty when it is part of the meaning.

Prefer:

"I'm not sure if it works."

not:

"This should work, although results may vary."

Prefer:

"Is that realistic?"

not:

"Could you please clarify whether this hypothesis is technically plausible?"

## Avoid generic executive compression

Do not transform a real account of what happened into abstract corporate summary language unless the user explicitly requests a summary.

## Avoid unnecessary lexical sophistication

C1 English does not mean using the fanciest possible vocabulary. Prefer the simplest precise word.

## Human variation

Allow normal variation in sentence length. A short sentence can follow a long one. Not every paragraph needs a topic sentence. Not every message needs a polished opener and closer.
